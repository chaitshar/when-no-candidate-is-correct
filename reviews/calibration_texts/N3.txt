Affiliation: Palaestra Research Email: elasky2024@berkeley.edu Frank Nakasako 1 1 footnotemark: 1 Affiliation: Palaestra Research Email: frank@palaestraresearch.org Naman Goyal Affiliation: Independent Abstract Despite theoretical promise, debate as a scalable oversight protocol has produced mixed empirical results: gains in some settings, and null effects in others, especially when the judge does not have information hidden from it. We study proposer-critic debate in a stronger-debater/weaker-judge setting on programmatically verifiable code and logic tasks. Debate helps the judge over a consultancy baseline when the critic provides a usable advantage: the critic’s classification ability must exceed the judge’s, and the judge must treat critic speeches as claims to verify rather than testimony to summarize. On the three of five pairings where the condition holds, proposer-critic debate’s gains are statistically significant over consultancy, and these pairings are the most capable model pairings. On the two non-responder pairings in our set, debate produces null effects, and judge verification rates drop by tens of percentage points once a critic enters the transcript. In these cases the critic’s binary-classification ability and the judge’s are within noise of each other, and the critic’s disagreement is parsed as testimony rather than a claim to check. Ablating rebuttal rounds from debate produces no measurable change in judge performance: a single independent critique recovers the bulk of debate’s benefit at lower inference cost. These findings suggest a cheaper primitive for training-free scalable oversight in verifiable domains (answer, critique, judge) and a pre-deployment audit (does the critic beat the judge, and will the judge verify it?) that predicts when debate will help. 1 Introduction State-of-the-art training regimes enable LLM performance rivalling the best humans on verifiable domains ( OpenAI, 2024 ; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ) . Frontier and open-weight models now succeed at complicated programming tasks ( Jimenez et al., 2024 ) , broad knowledge tasks ( Wang et al., 2024 ) , and graduate-level science reasoning ( Rein et al., 2023 ) , and only the hardest single-turn programmatically verifiable benchmarks remain unsaturated ( Phan et al., 2025 ; Glazer et al., 2024 ; Chollet et al., 2025 ) . However, fuzzier domains, whose outcomes are harder to verify programmatically, such as creative writing and scientific experiment design, lag behind ( Xu et al., 2025 ; Rashkin et al., 2025 ; Si et al., 2025 ) . Post-training in these areas relies substantially on human expert labels and/or LLM ratings for reward assignment ( Moonshot AI, 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) , typically via reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF; Christiano et al. 2017 ) or AI feedback (RLAIF; Bai et al. 2022 ). Both approaches have persistent problems. Human labels remain expensive and are typically collected offline ( Ouyang et al., 2022 ; Casper et al., 2023 ) , which delays training and can make supervision increasingly off-policy. Machine labels suffer from quality issues and adversarial brittleness, failing to provide a fully stable training signal ( Casper et al., 2023 ; Wen et al., 2024 ) ; and methods that mitigate these issues often reintroduce human experts through rubric design and data curation ( Moonshot AI, 2025 ) . Prior work proposes debate as a potential alternative, with theoretical results suggesting that debate causes strong arguments to surface ( Irving et al., 2018 ; Brown-Cohen et al., 2023 ) . Compared with RLAIF-style baselines in which a model judge evaluates an answer directly or after one-sided self-defense, debate adds an independent critic who can surface falsifying evidence. Empirical findings have been uneven: Khan et al. (2024) found debate gains in hidden-evidence settings, while Kenton et al. (2024) reported weak or null effects in visible-information weak-judge settings closer to the one we study. We do not attempt to fully reconcile these results—protocol, task, and judge differences across that literature make a direct comparison difficult, and our diagnostic does not cleanly translate to two-debater assigned-position protocols. We instead identify a measurable condition under which proposer–critic debate reliably helps in our setting, and we show, through transcript-level behavior reviews, why it does. On code and logic—calibrated verifiable testbeds chosen so that we can audit reward signal against ground truth—proposer–critic debate raises macro-F1 over consultancy on three of five model pairings and shows no effect on the other two. The split is governed by a single condition: debate helps only when the critic provides a usable advantage . That condition has two sub-parts, both of which must hold. First, the critic’s classifier macro-F1 must exceed the opening-only consultancy judge’s—there has to be a margin worth importing; raw judge capability and the critic’s generator–verifier gap do not predict the split, but this critic–judge classifier gap does. Second, the judge must treat the critic’s stance as a claim to verify rather than testimony to summarize. Behavior reviews of the transcripts (§ 4.2 , Appendix L ) show the mechanism: on responder pairings both sub-parts hold and judges import the critic’s signal; on the two non-responder pairings in our set, both sub-parts fail together—the critic’s and judge’s binary-classification ability are within noise of each other, and verification rates drop by tens of percentage points once a critic enters the transcript, with the critic’s disagreement parsed as testimony rather than a claim to check. We flag a small-N caveat: with five pairings, the gap diagnostic is a hypothesis well-supported within our experimental set, not a law established at scale. We also note that with ARC-AGI-2 substantially harder than Code Contests, our pairings do not let us cleanly separate the critic–judge gap from broader capability differences; the two conditions we identify (judge verification behavior and critic–judge classifier gap) are themselves capability properties, just more specific ones than raw solve rate. Whether the same mechanism transfers to fuzzier domains where programmatic verification is unavailable is a hypothesis warranting further work, not a finding from our experiments. Our main contributions are as follows: • In a visible-information weak-judge setting, proposer–critic debate lifts macro-F1 by 7 7 – 16 16 pp on three of five CodeContests+ and ARC-AGI-2 pairings, and gives no benefit on the other two. • A single condition governs when debate helps—the critic provides a usable advantage —with two sub-parts that must jointly hold: (i) the critic’s classifier macro-F1 exceeds the opening-only consultancy judge’s (the more obvious generator–verifier gap does not predict the split), and (ii) the judge treats the critic’s stance as a claim to verify rather than testimony to summarize. On the two non-responder pairings, both sub-parts fail together. • At test time, almost all of the lift comes from the critic’s opening stance; rebuttals neither help nor hurt. Their training-time value is out of scope here. 2 Background 2.1 Scalable Oversight A central challenge in scalable oversight is getting reliable reward signal when human supervision is scarce, limited, or less capable than the systems being supervised. Current post-training pipelines often use reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or AI feedback (RLAIF), but both can be unstable: human labels are expensive and often offline, while machine labels can be brittle or exploitable. Debate ( Irving et al., 2018 ) offers a different reward-labeling protocol. Instead of asking a judge to evaluate an answer in isolation, debate elicits adversarial information that may help a weaker judge decide whether a stronger model’s answer is correct. This framing connects to the broader scalable oversight literature. Cotra (2021) ’s sandwiching paradigm and Bowman et al. (2022) ’s question-answering experiments study whether weaker or non-expert supervisors can be aided by models to approximate expert judgment. Recent work extends this question to richer oversight protocols, including market making ( Hubinger, 2020 ) , and self-critique ( Saunders et al., 2022 ) . Model-generated critiques have been shown to help human evaluators find flaws in summaries and model-written code, though critics can hallucinate problems and therefore remain imperfect supervision aids ( Saunders et al., 2022 ; McAleese et al., 2024 ) . Prior work also finds that larger models produce outputs that are harder to critique while becoming better critics themselves ( Saunders et al., 2022 ) , motivating scale as a possible source of critic–judge gaps. More recently, Engels et al. (2025) establish scaling laws for scalable oversight across four games (mafia, debate, backdoor code, wargames), finding that debate has the highest nested-oversight success rate at a fixed Elo gap among the protocols tested, though all protocols’ rates decline as the overseen system grows stronger. We study a narrower and more training-relevant question: does debate give a weaker judge a better reward label than consultancy or answer-only evaluation when the answer comes from a stronger model? Our setting differs from prior scalable-oversight experiments in three ways. First, we evaluate debate as reward labeling, not mainly as human assistance or answer recovery. Second, the judge sees the problem and proposed answer; in debate it also sees the critic’s stance or argument. The challenge is not to recover hidden source material, but to verify or falsify a visible answer. Third, we use an open proposer–critic format. The proposer generates its own solution, and the critic may agree or disagree. This makes the critic closer to a verifier than an advocate, and makes the consultancy comparison cleaner: consultancy lacks a critic, but is not weakened by forced false advocacy ( Roger, 2024 ; Kenton et al., 2024 ) . 2.2 Debate We focus on judge-mediated debate as a scalable oversight protocol, distinct from multi-agent “debate” methods that use round-robin discussion and majority voting to improve factuality or reasoning ( Du et al., 2023 ; Liang et al., 2023 ) . In judge-mediated debate, participants present arguments to a judge whose task is to decide which answer or claim is correct. Irving et al. (2018) provide an early formulation of debate for scalable oversight, supported by image-classification experiments in which debaters reveal information that helps a limited judge classify images more accurately. Empirical debate work often studies cases where the judge lacks decisive evidence. In hidden-passage QuALITY experiments, debate can beat consultancy by surfacing passage evidence for the judge ( Pang et al., 2021 ; Michael et al., 2023 ; Khan et al., 2024 ) . That advantage weakens when the passage is visible, which suggests that some gains come from evidence retrieval rather than better verification ( Khan et al., 2024 ; Parrish et al., 2022a ; Parrish et al., 2022b ) . Kenton et al. (2024) extend this line to weak-judge/strong-agent settings in mathematics, coding, logic, and multimodal reasoning, and find smaller debate gains over consultancy. This distinction is central here: in code and ARC-AGI-style tasks, the judge sees the task and candidate answer. The question is whether a critic helps the judge check that answer. A second difference is stance assignment. Some debate and consultancy comparisons force agents to defend externally specified positions. This can make consultancy artificially weak when the consultant must defend an answer it would not endorse. Kenton et al. (2024) ’s open-debate setting lets one debater choose its position while fixing the other in opposition. We go further: the proposer generates an answer, and the critic decides whether to agree or disagree. Open consultancy is therefore a cleaner baseline: it lacks a critic, but is not penalized by forced false advocacy ( Roger, 2024 ) . Theoretical work studies when debate can provide robust oversight guarantees. Brown-Cohen et al. (2023) introduce doubly-efficient debate, designing protocols in which the honest debater requires only polynomial computation while the dishonest debater gains no advantage from exponential resources. Brown-Cohen et al. (2025) further address the obfuscated-arguments problem through prover-estimator debate, which aims to reduce the risk of arguments that are persuasive but difficult for the judge to evaluate. We distinguish our empirical protocol from these theoretical variants, which address formal concerns about computational asymmetries, stochastic systems, and obfuscated arguments. Our reward-labeling framing is relevant to safety-case arguments for debate. Buhl et al. (2025) sketch an alignment safety case in which debate training helps produce honest systems. That argument depends on debate supplying a trusted reward signal, plus further assumptions about convergence, exploration, and obfuscated arguments. We do not test that full safety case. We test a narrower prerequisite: in verifiable domains, does debate give a weaker judge a more accurate correctness signal than consultancy or answer-only evaluation? 2.3 LLM-as-a-Judge and Prover-Verifier Games Our use of weaker LLMs as judges of stronger participants connects to a growing literature on LLM-based evaluation. Zheng et al. (2023) show that strong LLM judges can approximate human preferences with over 80% agreement, but identify systematic failure modes including position bias, verbosity bias, and self-enhancement bias—all of which are also relevant when LLM judges evaluate debate transcripts. Burns et al. (2024) demonstrate more broadly that weak model supervision can elicit strong model capabilities, establishing that the weak-to-strong oversight paradigm underlying our judge setup is viable in principle. In a complementary line of work, Kirchner et al. (2024) use adversarial prover-verifier games to improve the legibility of LLM outputs, iteratively training provers whose solutions a small verifier can evaluate. While prover-verifier games share debate’s intuition that adversarial interaction improves oversight, they operate in a single-agent setting and optimize for reasoning legibility rather than conclusion truthfulness. 3 Methods 3.1 Protocols We measure reward signal by asking whether a judge correctly labels a proposer’s answer as correct or incorrect. Following Khan et al. (2024) ; Kenton et al. (2024) , we compare debate to consultancy and several ablations. Unlike earlier debate work, we do not force participants to defend positions they believe are false; both debaters argue for their own positions from the start. This extends Kenton et al. (2024) ’s “open debate,” which fixes one debater in opposition. Proposer-critic debate. We use proposer-critic debate ( Elasky and Nakasako, 2026 ) : a proposer generates and argues for a solution, and a critic agrees or disagrees with it. Debaters cannot switch positions mid-round, and both can win or lose. The judge then renders a verdict on the proposer’s solution. Open consultancy. Our primary baseline is open consultancy ( Roger, 2024 ; Kenton et al., 2024 ) : the proposer generates a solution and then argues for it, but there is no critic. Our main comparisons use one proposer, which isolates the critic’s contribution. Preliminary double-consultancy ablations in Appendix E test whether a second independent consultant closes the gap by matching answer-generation compute. Some prior work lets the judge ask questions between rounds ( Kenton et al., 2024 ) ; preliminary Qwen3.5-122B experiments found no significant accuracy effect, consistent with Khan et al. (2024) , so we omit this for simplicity. Openings-only variants. Openings-only debate and openings-only consultancy remove rebuttal speeches: the judge sees only the initial position(s) (proposer’s solution plus, in debate, the critic’s stance) and classifies correctness. These ablations isolate the value of rebuttals. Direct QA. Although not an oversight protocol, Direct QA —directly asking a model to solve a task—provides a proxy for task difficulty. Figure 1 : Consultancy vs. debate protocols. Both modes use the same proposer answer generation; debate adds a critic and passes the full transcript to the judge. In debate, the critic also precomputes an agree / disagree stance against the proposer’s answer before any speeches. Mapping to RLAIF baselines. We treat these protocols as a ladder of RLAIF-style reward labelers. Direct QA asks the judge model to solve the task itself; it measures task difficulty rather than classification over a candidate answer. The closest bare LLM-as-reward-model baseline is opening-only consultancy: the judge sees the problem and the proposer’s generated answer, but no advocacy transcript. Full consultancy adds one-sided defense of that same answer. These baselines grow stronger, but remain one-sided: the judge sees no independent adversarial signal. Debate adds that signal through the critic. Our main question is whether this helps the same weak judge produce a more accurate reward label. 3.2 Data We focus on single-turn verifiable tasks because correctness is most directly measurable. After surveying available benchmarks—most of which were either saturated or noisily labeled—we selected the hardest single-turn verifiable datasets we could find for binary correctness classification. Code. We use CodeContests+ ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , a re-test-cased competitive-programming dataset built on top of CodeContests, with LLM-generated test cases that raise true-positive rates and reduce false positives over the original benchmark. We subsample 1000 problems and filter timeouts, leaving 976. Logic. ARC-AGI-2 ( Chollet et al., 2025 ) assesses fluid intelligence through abstract reasoning. A submission is correct iff it matches the hidden target grid exactly. We use the 120-problem evaluation set. 3.3 Format Each protocol begins with one answer generation round. Each format then has two speech rounds, consultancy with one speaker and debate with two. Debate’s speech rounds are simultaneous: at turn N N , no participant sees another’s turn- N N response. In other words, in each round, both participants speak at the same time and share the same argument context, which contains answers and speeches made in prior rounds. In debate, the proposer generates first, and the critic conditions its agree/disagree stance on that answer. Additional generation controls appear in Appendix A.4 . 3.4 Model Selection To test whether weaker models can supervise stronger ones, we pair stronger debaters with smaller judges across open-source and frontier model families. We call the first group responder pairings and the second non-responder pairings . Two patterns drive the result. First, when debate helps, it mostly cuts false positives: judges stop endorsing wrong proposer answers. Second, debate helps only when the critic’s classifier signal beats the judge’s answer-only baseline and the judge checks that signal rather than merely summarizing it. Debate’s gains concentrate on rejecting wrong proposers. On the three responder pairings, moving from consultancy to debate cuts the judge’s false positive rate (endorsing an incorrect proposer answer) by 7.3 7.3 – 10.0 10.0 pp, while the false negative rate stays roughly flat (Appendix L , Table 16 ). This asymmetry matters for reward modeling: false positives teach a policy that flawed reasoning is acceptable, while false negatives only withhold reward from good answers. Consultancy raises agreement with the proposer indiscriminately. The critic gives the judge concrete grounds to disagree—a held-out-grid inconsistency on ARC, a counterexample input on code—without inducing fabricated objections to correct answers. Class-conditional F1 shows the same pattern (Figure 3 ). Incorrect-class F1 rises by + 20.8 +20.8 , + 24.7 +24.7 , and + 23.7 +23.7 pp on Gemini, Opus, and Qwen, while correct-class F1 rises only + 4 +4 – 7 7 pp. We use class-F1 because it is symmetric in the two error types and matches the metric used for protocol evaluation. Opus 4.5 has the opposite no-transcript prior (its baseline tendency on the proposer’s answer alone, with no advocacy) from the other two responder judges—it under-endorses correct proposers, where Qwen and Gemini over-endorse—so its largest raw verdict-count movement is accepting correct answers rather than rejecting wrong ones (Appendix D , Table 5 ).
