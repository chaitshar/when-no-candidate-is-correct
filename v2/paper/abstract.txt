Many AI systems write several candidate answers and then ask an AI judge to pick the best one. But sometimes every candidate is wrong, and the right move is to pick none. We study this case. We compare two ways of judging. In PICK, the judge sees all candidates together and picks one or says NONE. In ISO, the judge checks each candidate alone. Our idea is simple: seeing the candidates together makes the judge more willing to choose, and this hurts most on problems the judge cannot solve itself. For claude-haiku-5-5 on hard math problems, PICK gives more wrong picks than ISO, and the gap stays in three checks that we fixed in advance: giving PICK three calls so that both methods use the same compute (0.632 vs 0.353 wrong picks), removing the judge's own earlier answer (gap +0.263), and removing the candidate it picked first (0.558 vs 0.256). For other judges the results are mixed. On 100 math problems, gpt-oss-120b-medium and gemini-3.8-flash-medium are more willing to choose under PICK overall, but for the first one wrong picks do not go up, because it already accepts many wrong answers when it checks them alone; both judges also miss our minimum group size. On science ideas from 10 ResearchBench papers, PICK gives more wrong picks for Haiku and gpt-oss-120b-medium, but for three Gemini settings it mostly gives more right picks. We tried three fixes. Two fail on hard math. The third, checking each candidate alone first and picking only among the accepted ones, has the same wrong-pick rate as ISO (0.175 vs 0.625 for PICK) and costs about three times the calls.
